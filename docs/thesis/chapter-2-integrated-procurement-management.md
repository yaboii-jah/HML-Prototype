# Integrated Procurement Management

Integrated procurement management concerns the coordination of requirements, supplier selection, purchasing decisions, and fulfillment information across related activities. Chan and Owusu's (2022) review presents procurement as a sequence extending from defining requirements to selecting sources, establishing agreements, fulfilling orders, and closing transactions. Alabdali and Salam (2022) likewise examine procurement through information exchanges involving demand, suppliers, and purchasing histories. These perspectives provide the conceptual basis for the present study, which proposes linking material request forms (MRFs), managerial review, supplier quotations, purchase orders, and supporting records within a shared workflow. The relevant similarity is the continuity of information between procurement stages, with each purchasing decision remaining associated with the requirement it addresses ([Chan & Owusu, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings12020198); [Alabdali & Salam, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/su141912269)).

This continuity becomes more specific when procurement activities are examined at the level of information requirements. Alabdali and Salam's (2022) survey instrument includes supplier selection, collection of procurement demand, communication of requirements, and electronic purchasing histories. These activities correspond to the present study's approved material requests, quotation comparison, and retained transaction records. Their survey of 221 supply-chain professionals, predominantly based in Saudi Arabia, identified positive modeled relationships among digital transformation, procurement, and competitive advantage. This organizational evidence complements Chan and Owusu's (2022) review of 64 publications by connecting digital procurement practices with broader organizational outcomes. However, the survey does not test the proposed application, while the review explicitly identifies the need for empirical assessment. Their contribution to the present study therefore lies in identifying relevant procurement activities; the functionality of the proposed integration requires assessment in the intended setting ([Alabdali & Salam, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/su141912269); [Chan & Owusu, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings12020198)).

Related procurement studies further illustrate the connection between material requests, purchasing controls, and supporting records. Tabangay and Siddayao (2024) developed a university procurement management system with purchase request approval, supplier quotations, quotation summaries, purchase orders, budget affirmation, order approval, and inspection and acceptance functions. These documented functions closely resemble the present study's request validation, comparative quotation review, purchase order preparation, budget verification, and receiving records. The similarity concerns how a request passes through related decisions and documents, rather than the mere use of computerized forms. Magdaraog (2023), examining procurement in three Department of Public Works and Highways district offices in Masbate, recommended procedural checklists, budget review, and secure storage of critical documents. Read together, these studies support the proposed association of purchasing records with approvals and supporting evidence. Their institutional procedures nevertheless require adaptation to the present study's private construction setting ([Tabangay & Siddayao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.56648/aide-irj.v8i1.115); [Magdaraog, 2023](https://ijeber.com/uploads2023/ijeber_03_109.pdf)).

Coordination across projects introduces a distinction between sharing procurement information and concentrating purchasing authority. Through ten expert interviews, Sivasubramaniam et al. (2024) identified potential advantages of centralized construction procurement, including monitoring multiple projects and supporting informed decisions. Participants also identified difficulties satisfying individual project requirements. Gurgun et al. (2024) similarly identified internal integration as a consideration in introducing electronic procurement into construction supply chains. For the present study, these findings suggest a design principle: common records should retain project-specific quantities, specifications, and assigned responsibilities. Shared visibility would allow personnel to review requests across sites while preserving the source project and approval history of each transaction. This is an application of the literature to information coordination; it does not establish that the proposed system adopts the centralized purchasing model examined in Sri Lanka or achieves its anticipated benefits ([Sivasubramaniam et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.4038/faruj.v11i1.313); [Gurgun et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings14030605)).

Maintaining those project references is particularly relevant when procurement requirements change. Gurgun et al. (2024) examined 28 integration barriers using 131 valid practitioner questionnaires, barrier-ranking analysis, and 15 interviews. Unexpected cancellations, material-price variation, and changing construction requirements emerged as leading concerns. These findings extend the coordination issue identified by Sivasubramaniam et al. (2024): shared procedures must still accommodate differing and changing project needs. The present study responds through a proposed sourcing workflow in which an approved MRF may be fulfilled partly from another site's available materials and partly through supplier purchases. Keeping both allocations associated with the original request would allow personnel to distinguish transfer quantities from quantities requiring quotation and purchase order processing. This is a context-specific design response, rather than a feature evaluated by either study; their findings do not establish that the proposed allocation process will eliminate procurement disruptions ([Gurgun et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings14030605); [Sivasubramaniam et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.4038/faruj.v11i1.313)).

Implementation evidence helps distinguish this proposed functionality from established operational outcomes. Parisi and Donyavi (2026) evaluated a technology-integration framework across six construction small and medium-sized enterprises using cases, interviews, surveys, and observations. Their combination of mobile applications, RFID, and cloud-based systems reportedly reduced average order-processing time by 34%, with phased implementation and feedback supporting adjustment to project and supplier requirements. The construction setting and coordination of material procurement provide a closer operational comparison than the university setting of Tabangay and Siddayao (2024). However, the present study relies on personnel-entered records and authorized review, rather than the same combined technologies. Parisi and Donyavi also acknowledge a restricted case sample and the absence of a control group. Their reported reduction consequently provides evidence from the studied framework, while the present study's functional and user assessments cannot establish an equivalent processing-time improvement ([Parisi & Donyavi, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15623599.2025.2553610); [Tabangay & Siddayao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.56648/aide-irj.v8i1.115)).

The Philippine studies further clarify the distinction between assessing procurement practices and evaluating a developed system. Magdaraog (2023) used a descriptive-inferential survey of 55 respondents to assess pre-procurement, procurement, and post-procurement practices and inform a proposed digital process. Tabangay and Siddayao (2024), in contrast, surveyed employees after system deployment using ISO 25010-based criteria, reporting an overall weighted mean of 4.42 and a functional suitability rating of 4.53. The latter approach is more directly comparable to the present study's planned functionality and user assessment, whereas Magdaraog provides guidance on procedural and documentary requirements. Neither type of assessment should be treated as a direct measurement of reduced purchasing time or costs. Together, they support examining whether the proposed system performs its intended procurement functions and meets users' needs ([Magdaraog, 2023](https://ijeber.com/uploads2023/ijeber_03_109.pdf); [Tabangay & Siddayao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.56648/aide-irj.v8i1.115)).

The reviewed evidence leaves a bounded contextual gap: conceptual procurement models require empirical assessment, construction integration findings remain dependent on their implementation settings, and local system evaluation concerns a university workflow ([Chan & Owusu, 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings12020198); [Parisi & Donyavi, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15623599.2025.2553610); [Tabangay & Siddayao, 2024](https://doi.org/10.56648/aide-irj.v8i1.115)). The present study addresses this gap through the development and functional and user assessment of connected request review, sourcing, quotation comparison, and purchasing records for a private construction organization operating across project sites. Its contribution is a contextual implementation and assessment of that workflow, with effectiveness claims limited to the evidence its evaluation produces.
